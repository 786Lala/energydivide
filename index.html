How the halves are defined: the global population is ranked by per-capita primary energy use and split into two equal halves — not by national income. The rich half includes most OECD countries, China and other high-consumption economies; the poor half includes much of South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.       02 · The divide

      One planet, two energy lifestyles

      The gap is not only about how much energy is used. It shows up in electricity, clean cooking, materials and carbon — and it has narrowed far too slowly.

      2023 snapshot

      The rich half's share of global energy

      Share of world primary energy consumed by the richest 50%.

      86% → 78%

      The divide is narrowing — but at this pace, the rich half would still consume more than half of the world's energy in 2050.

      The per-person energy gap

      How many times more energy the average rich-half person uses.

      6.1× → 3.5×

      Catching up is real, but slow: a person in the rich half still uses more than three times the energy of a person in the poor half.

      The disparity in one sentence

      A person in the rich half uses 3.5× the primary energy, 9.3× the electricity and 5× the materials of a person in the poor half — while 2.3 billion people still cook with wood, charcoal or dung.

        How the forecast model works

        Each year, per-person energy use compounds at the chosen demand growth rate, minus efficiency gains, plus the effect of selected interventions. Population grows at the chosen rate and is split equally between the two halves. Energy totals are per-person use × population; CO₂ is energy × the carbon intensity of each half, which falls faster when clean-energy policies are selected.

        The dashed “current trajectory” holds today's rates constant: +0.4%/yr rich-half demand, +1.6%/yr poor-half demand, 0.4%/yr efficiency and 1.0%/yr decarbonisation. It is a trend extrapolation, not a prediction. All figures are illustrative and rounded for teaching purposes.

        04 · Closing the gap

        Steps that would actually narrow it

        No single lever is enough. The gap closes fastest when high-consuming economies consume less, clean energy reaches everyone, and materials circulate for longer. Add any step to the forecast to see its effect.
